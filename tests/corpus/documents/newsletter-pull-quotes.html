A new orchard for the east meadowAfter two years of planning, forty fruit trees go into the ground in April

When the association bought the strip of meadow behind plot row K in 2025, nobody quite agreed what to do with it. Some wanted more allotments, others a car park, and a vocal minority argued for leaving it entirely alone. The compromise that emerged from three general meetings and an unusually long survey is a community orchard: forty trees of old local varieties, a wildflower understorey and a path that is wide enough for wheelbarrows and wheelchairs alike.

The trees come from the regional pomology society, which grafted them last winter from cuttings collected in village gardens around the river valley. Among them are apples such as the Riverside Pippin, which was thought lost until a member found a single tree behind a barn in 2019, as well as pears, plums, two quinces and a medlar.

“We are planting trees that our grandchildren will harvest. That is the whole point.”Marta Lindgren, orchard working group

Planting will take place on the weekends of 10 and 17 April. Every tree needs a hole of about a metre across, a stake, a rabbit guard and a good bucket of compost from our own heaps. Volunteers are welcome for an hour or a whole day; tools, gloves and soup will be provided. Children can help with labelling the trees and sowing the meadow mix along the path.

Looking after an orchard does not end with planting. In the first three summers the young trees need regular watering, and a rota will be posted in the clubhouse. Pruning courses are planned for the winter, led by members of the pomology society who have kindly offered their time.

Who pays for it?

The trees, stakes and guards cost a little over 3,200 euros. Half is covered by a grant from the district’s climate fund, the rest by the association’s reserves and by donations from members, who can sponsor a tree for 40 euros and choose its name tag.

Water, slugs and the dry summerWhat we learned from the hottest season on record

Last year’s drought tested everyone. The two main water tanks ran dry twice in August, and the association had to buy in water from the town for the first time in its history. The board has since applied for permission to install a third tank fed from the clubhouse roof, which would add about twelve cubic metres of storage.

“Mulch everything. Then mulch it again.”Advice from plot 23

Members who coped best shared a few habits: they mulched heavily with grass clippings and straw, watered deeply twice a week instead of a little every day, and grouped thirsty crops together so that the watering can went where it was needed. Several also experimented with buried clay pots, the so-called ollas, which slowly release water into the surrounding soil.

Slug patrol

Beer traps work, but they also catch beetles that eat slugs. Try boards laid on the soil instead: check them in the morning and move the slugs to the hedge at the far end of the site, well away from the beds.

Slugs, meanwhile, had a quieter year than usual because of the dry weather, but the wet spring forecast suggests they will be back in force. The hedgehog houses built by the youth group last autumn are now occupied, which is the best news this newsletter has to report.

Finally, a reminder that hoses with spray nozzles are still not permitted during the summer months. Please use watering cans or the drip lines that can be borrowed from the tool shed against a small deposit.
